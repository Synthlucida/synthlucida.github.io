Chord functions within a scale

Every scale degree belongs to one of three groups: tonic (rest, "home"), subdominant (moving away, tension building) and dominant (pull back to the tonic). A progression that cycles through these groups in a sensible order — T → S → D → T — always sounds coherent, whatever the exact chords are.

Circle of fifths

The outer ring shows the 12 major keys arranged by fifths, the inner ring their relative minors. Neighboring keys (one step left or right) share 6 of 7 scale tones, so it's easy to modulate between them or "borrow" a chord from a neighbor. Hover or tap a key to highlight its neighbors.

Keys opposite each other on the circle (e.g. C and F#) share the fewest common tones and sound the most contrasting together — useful for a sudden modulation or a shift between sections of a song.

How it works

01

Pick a key and mood

The key sets the starting note; the mood (happy, sad, dreamy, epic, chill/lo-fi, dark, romantic, nostalgic) sets the major or minor mode, tempo, and typical character of the progression.

02

The tool builds chords only from the scale

The progression is built exclusively from chords diatonic to the chosen key and scale — no random or foreign notes. Most chords are simple triads or 7th chords, but some moods also weave in an occasional sus2, sus4, or 9th color chord for extra character — always still built purely from the scale, so it never sounds out of tune.

03

Melody, bass, arp, and drums are derived from the chords

The melody draws on the tones of the current chord (triad/seventh chord) in rhythmic patterns, the bassline plays the root, fifth, and octave, the arp cycles through the chord's tones in eighth-note steps following the chosen pattern, and the drums add kick, snare, and hi-hat in a sixteenth-note groove following the chosen style — all four always stay in sync with the chords above. Pick a specific melody, bass, arp, or drum style from the dropdowns, or leave them on "Auto" to get a different pattern on every bar.

04

Fine-tune the volume and the notes

The "Instrument mixer" panel balances the volume of chords, melody, bass, arp and drums independently. The "Mini DAW" panel then lets you pick any bar and mute individual melody, bass, arp or drum steps, shift a note up or down an octave, or just re-roll that one bar if it isn't working — the rest of the progression stays untouched. Every edit updates playback and the MIDI export immediately.

05

Play it, or export it

The Play button starts an 8-bar loop right in your browser — the whole mix, or just one layer at a time (chords only / melody only / bass only / arp only / drums only). MIDI export downloads a .mid file with five tracks (chords / melody / bass / arp / drums) that you can open in any DAW and build on — or download a single layer on its own. Everything you generate here is yours to use without restriction — in personal or commercial tracks, with no attribution required.

Tip: "New variation" keeps the key and mood unchanged and just rolls a different progression from the same family — handy when you like the mood but want to try different chords. "New melody" and "New bass" re-roll just that part while keeping the current chords in place, so you can mix and match. The exported MIDI file can be freely transposed in your DAW, looped longer, or have its sounds swapped for your own.

Frequently asked questions

Is the chord generator free?

Yes, the tool is completely free and runs right in your browser, no sign-up needed.

Are the generated progressions musically correct?

Yes — every progression is built solely from chords diatonic to the chosen key and scale, so it always sounds coherent and "in tune." The melody and bassline are generated from the tones of those chords.

What format is the export?

The export is a standard MIDI file (.mid, format 1, 480 tpqn) with five tracks — chords, melody, bass, arp, and drums — compatible with all major DAWs.

Which moods can I choose from?

Happy, sad, dreamy, epic, chill/lo-fi, dark, romantic, and nostalgic — each mood has its own tempo, key mode, and set of typical progressions.

Can I use the exported MIDI files without restriction?

Yes. Any MIDI file you generate and download here is yours to use freely — in personal or commercial music, with no license fees, credit, or attribution required.

Can I set the volume of each instrument separately?

Yes — the "Instrument mixer" panel lets you set the volume of chords, melody, bass, arp and drums independently, so you can tuck a layer back or bring it forward before you play or export.

Can I edit individual notes after generating a progression?

Yes, in the "Mini DAW" panel. Pick a bar and mute individual melody, bass, arp or drum steps, shift a note up or down an octave, pick a completely different note for any step, or re-roll just that one bar. Edits show up in playback and the MIDI export right away.

Can I export the progression as sheet music?

Yes — the "Sheet music (PDF)" button exports the current progression as standard music notation (treble and bass clef, chord symbols above the staff) into a PDF you can print or send along. Below the notation, each bar also gets a piano key diagram with the keys marked, so it works even if you can't read notes. It reflects any edits made in the Mini DAW too.

Articles & tips for making music

How to write a good chord progression, even without theory

Most songs you hear on the radio rest on a handful of simple principles: the progression stays in one key, alternates tonic and dominant function, and only occasionally borrows one extra "color" chord. That's why even a completely amateur progression can sound great as long as it sticks to the scale — and why even a technically correct one sounds off the moment it strays outside it. The fastest way to a usable progression is to start from the mood, not the chords: decide whether you want the track to feel happy, sad, dreamy, or epic, and let that mood dictate the key (major vs. minor) and the tempo. Only then worry about the specific chords — or let the generator pick them and try a few variations until one matches what you're going for.

Song mood: how key and scale shape emotion

A major scale generally reads to listeners as bright, confident, and open — which is why it anchors happy, epic, and nostalgic tracks. A minor scale carries tension and introspection instead, which is why it works for sad, dark, and dreamy moods. Tempo then fills in the intensity: a slow 60–75 BPM deepens melancholy or dreaminess, while 100+ BPM gives a progression energy and urgency. "Minor key + slow tempo + simple triads" is the classic recipe for a ballad, while "major key + mid tempo + seventh chords" shows up constantly in lo-fi and chillhop. Try the same key across a few moods in the generator above and you'll hear the difference immediately.

What to do with the exported MIDI file (DAW workflow)

Drag the exported .mid file onto an empty MIDI track in Ableton, FL Studio, Logic, or Reaper — it contains four separate tracks (chords, melody, bass, arpeggio), so you can assign each one its own instrument or VST plugin. A fast starting point: put a pad or piano on the chord track, a simple synth bass on the bassline, and let the melody play through a lead or pluck synth. If the loop feels short, just copy and paste the whole block, or tweak individual notes directly in the piano-roll editor — the generated progression is only a starting point you're free to keep shaping. Downloaded files carry no license or watermark, so you can drop them straight into a commercial project too.

Progrese's eight moods and what sets them apart

The tool offers eight built-in moods, each combining a different key mode, tempo, and set of typical progressions. Happy is major and faster (around 112 BPM), built on classic pop progressions. Sad and dark are both minor, but sad stays more melodic while dark leans on tenser jumps between chords. Dreamy and romantic both add seventh or ninth chords for a fuller, softer sound and play at a slow tempo. Epic is minor and dramatic, sitting around 90 BPM, while chill/lo-fi is also minor with seventh chords but a more relaxed groove. Nostalgic is the only major-key mood among the slower/mid-tempo group, and leans on the vi–IV pull that's characteristic of that sound. Moods can be switched anytime while keeping the same key, so it's easy to compare how one root note sounds across all eight.

Why export chords, melody, and bass separately

Alongside exporting the whole progression at once (five tracks in a single .mid file), you can also download just one layer — chords, melody, bass, arpeggio, or drums on its own. That's useful when you only want part of the generated idea: laying the bassline under your own chords, for example, or keeping the chords while writing your own melody and bass from scratch. Separate tracks are also easier to hand off between instruments or collaborators, since nobody has to figure out what to mute in the project. Because every layer comes from the same chord progression, they all stay harmonically compatible once split apart, so you can mix and match them with other instruments or sounds without worrying they'll clash.
