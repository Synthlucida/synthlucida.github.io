    What is SEO, and why does it matter

    SEO (search engine optimization) is a set of practices that make a website easier for Google and other search engines to understand, so they can index it correctly and show it to people searching for what you offer. It isn't a trick or a one-time fix — it's ongoing care of a site's technical foundation, content and structure.

    A site with solid SEO tends to load quickly, works on mobile, has clearly named pages, and content that genuinely matches what people are searching for. A site without those foundations can still be beautiful and useful, but search engines may simply never find it.

    Takeaway

    SEO doesn't decide how a site looks — it decides whether anyone finds it at all.

    The title tag: the first line anyone sees

    The title tag is the page name shown as the clickable headline in search results and as the tab title in a browser. It's the first thing a person notices in the results, and search engines treat it as a strong signal of what the page is about.

    The ideal length is roughly 30–65 characters — shorter wastes the space, longer gets cut off in results. A good title states the page's main topic and is unique for every page on the site.

    Takeaway

    Every page deserves its own clear title — it's the page's business card for both people and algorithms.

    The meta description: the text that decides the click

    The meta description is the short text under the title tag in search results. Google increasingly replaces it with its own snippet, but a well-written description that matches the content still has a good chance of showing up — and of getting read and clicked.

    The ideal length is 70–160 characters. It works best when it clearly summarizes the page's value, not as a pile of keywords.

    Takeaway

    A meta description doesn't directly affect ranking, but it strongly affects how many people click through.

    Heading structure (H1–H6): why the order matters

    Headings H1 through H6 create a hierarchy on the page, much like an outline in a document. There should be exactly one H1 summarizing the page's main topic, with further headings (H2, H3…) breaking the content into logical sections.

    Both search engines and screen readers rely on heading structure to understand what's the main topic and what's supporting detail. A page with no headings, or a chaotic one with several H1s, is harder for both to read.

    Takeaway

    Well-structured headings help search engines understand content faster, and help people navigate the page more easily.

    Mobile display: why the viewport tag can't be missing

    Most visits to a website today come from mobile, and Google has long evaluated the mobile version of a page first (mobile-first indexing). The viewport meta tag tells the browser how to render the page on a small screen — without it, the layout can shrink down to something unreadable and unusable.

    Beyond the viewport itself, it's worth checking font size, spacing between tappable buttons, and whether content overflows the screen.

    Takeaway

    If a page doesn't work well on mobile, it will show up in search results regardless of how good the content is.

    HTTPS: security that's also an SEO signal

    HTTPS encrypts the traffic between a browser and a server, so nobody along the way can eavesdrop on or alter the data. Beyond security, it's also one of the signals Google factors into ranking, and browsers actively flag non-HTTPS sites as "not secure", which discourages visitors.

    Practically every host offers HTTPS for free today (for example via Let's Encrypt), so there's little reason to go without it.

    Takeaway

    HTTPS is the baseline today, not a bonus — its absence makes both people and search engines wary.

    Image alt text: accessibility and SEO at once

    The alt attribute describes an image's content as text — screen readers use it for blind users, browsers show it if the image fails to load, and search engines read it since they can't see the image itself.

    Good alt text is short and descriptive, not stuffed with keywords. Purely decorative images can carry an empty alt="" attribute, which tells screen readers to skip right over them.

    Takeaway

    Alt text isn't just an SEO detail — it's real help for people who can't see the image.

    Open Graph: how your link looks when shared

    Open Graph tags (og:title, og:description, og:image) control how a page's preview looks when shared on Facebook, LinkedIn or elsewhere. Without them, the platform has to guess — often picking a random image or showing no card at all.

    A filled-in, visually clear preview raises the odds that people notice it in their feed and click through.

    Takeaway

    Open Graph doesn't affect search ranking, but it directly affects how well your content spreads on social platforms.

    Structured data: how to earn rich results

    Structured data is code — usually in JSON-LD format — that tells search engines exactly what a page contains: a star-rated review, a recipe, a Q&A, business details and so on.

    That can earn a richer, more visually distinctive result in search (a "rich snippet"), which draws more attention than a plain blue link. It's not a ranking guarantee, but it does increase visibility.

    Takeaway

    Structured data helps search engines understand a page precisely, instead of just guessing from the text.

    robots.txt and sitemap.xml: how search engines find you

    robots.txt tells search engines which parts of a site they may crawl and which they shouldn't. sitemap.xml works the other way — it's a map of the site, listing every important address you want search engines to know about and index.

    Both files usually live at the root of the domain, and robots.txt can point directly to the sitemap. Without them search engines will still find a site, just more slowly and less thoroughly.

    Takeaway

    Neither file is mandatory, but both make it much easier for search engines to find their way around a site.

    Internal links: why linking within your own site helps

    Internal links point from one page on your site to another page on the same site. They help people find related content, while also showing search engines which pages matter and how they relate to each other.

    A page nothing else links to is harder to discover and earns less trust. The basic rule: whenever you mention a topic you've covered in depth elsewhere on the site, link to it.

    Takeaway

    The more thoughtfully a site's pages are linked together, the easier it is for both people and search engines to navigate.

    How long do SEO changes take to show up?

    SEO isn't instant. Smaller technical fixes — title, meta description, alt text — can show up in search results within days to a few weeks, once a search engine recrawls and reassesses the page.

    Bigger changes — new content, building up a site's trustworthiness, restructuring — tend to take weeks to months to show. How fast it happens also depends on how often search engines crawl the site in the first place.

    Takeaway

    SEO is a long game — results build up gradually, not overnight.
